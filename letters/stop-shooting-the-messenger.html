Schools · November 12, 2026

Stop shooting the messenger

Dear Mayor Mamdani,

Every few years, someone at City Hall finds a new problem with the SHSAT. This fall it is Council Members Nantasha Williams and Lincoln Restler. In September they wrote to you and the Chancellor proposing that the test should no longer decide admission to five of the specialized high schools on its own. They want it weighed alongside grades, essays, teacher recommendations and “applicant backgrounds.” They also want Albany to repeal the law that keeps the test as the only way into Stuyvesant, Bronx Science and Brooklyn Tech.

My objection is simple: the test is not what is broken. It is the only part of the system honest enough to tell us that something is.

Consider what happened this August. Citywide reading proficiency in grades 3–8 fell six points, to about 50%. Third-grade reading fell 14 points. Math proficiency was flat, at 57%. Half of our children cannot read at grade level, and the Chancellor’s first response was to suggest that the problem might lie with the test itself.

The pattern is familiar. When a test delivers bad news, the response is to question the test.

Grades that hide the problem

The SHSAT is not the first test our children face. It is the first one with consequences. State tests in grades 3–8 show how many children are doing poorly, but by law, the state test results alone cannot determine whether a child moves to the next grade. So poor performance on these tests is mostly ignored. Report cards can be inflated, and the Regents exams are being dropped. The SHSAT ranks every applicant and shows objective results.

In this spring’s city school survey, 92% of third-grade parents said their child was progressing in reading as they expected. On the state test, 44% of third graders were proficient. Report cards and parent-teacher conferences are telling families that their children are on track. More than half are not.

The same gap appears at the end of high school. In 2024, 83% of city students graduated, but the State Comptroller found that only 52% were ready for college. Statewide, only 56% of students passed the Algebra I Regents exam last year, on a test where a passing score can require answering only about a third of the questions correctly. Albany’s response has been to lower the bar further: in September, the Board of Regents voted to begin removing the Regents exams as a graduation requirement. Today’s high school seniors will be the last class required to pass them.

What children are taught depends on where they live

Test scores track poverty closely. School by school, the correlation is strongly inverse, about −0.76: the poorer the school, the lower the scores. That is not a matter of ability. It is a matter of what children are taught. The specialized high schools expect students to arrive with algebra. Yet in 2024–25, about 8,000 more city students passed the state math test in seventh grade than were placed in Algebra I in eighth grade. In five districts, two in Manhattan, one in Brooklyn and two in the Bronx, fewer than half of the middle schools offer eighth-grade Algebra I at all. A child in one of those districts can do everything asked of her and still arrive at the SHSAT without the mathematics the specialized schools expect.

New York State spends more per student than any other state in the country. Money is not the problem. The SHSAT is simply the first place where the bill comes due.

We should be honest about what changing admissions would accomplish. Brooklyn Tech offers an aerospace engineering major. A student who cannot read at grade level or solve eighth-grade algebra will not be helped by being placed in it. That student will be set up for failure. The problem will not be solved, only hidden for four more years.

The Council letter also calls for a rigorous middle school math curriculum that prepares every student for Algebra I in eighth grade. Once that is in place, the test results may not be so alarming.

Please use your mayoral control to improve school performance in five simple steps:

  1. Bring in people who have already done it. Eva Moskowitz built Success Academy with children from the same neighborhoods, taking the same state tests, and its schools routinely outperform district schools. Across New York City charter schools in 2025, 68.6% of students were proficient in math, compared with 56.9% in district schools, according to the NYC Charter School Center. Among Black students, the gap was 68.7% to 43%. Invite Ms. Moskowitz and other charter founders to help redesign the system rather than treating them as adversaries.
  2. Make it possible to remove ineffective teachers. The current union contract makes accountability nearly impossible. A system that cannot part with people who do not teach will keep producing results it cannot explain. Make the authority to remove them your first priority in the next contract negotiation.
  3. Use third-party assessments every quarter to measure students’ true performance. By the time of the state tests at the end of the year, it is too late. External testing will ensure that underperforming teachers and administrators are caught while there is still time for children to get a real education, instead of losing an entire year.
  4. Partner with proven curricula such as Khan Academy, Art of Problem Solving or RSM to create rigorous curricula for our public schools. Tested materials that work already exist. There is no need to spend public funds and precious time reinventing the wheel.
  5. Use real textbooks and workbooks for the humanities and language arts, and require the teaching of grammar. A textbook helps students know what is coming and lets them go back and catch up if they were sick or absent. It also makes teaching much easier. We need higher standards for our public education.

The SHSAT is a thermometer. Breaking it because we dislike the reading will not cure the fever. The children who pay the price are the very children the Council says it wants to protect.

Sincerely,
Anya Levitov

What you can do

  • Ask your Council member not to support the Restler-Williams plan.
  • Tell your state senator and Assembly member that you oppose repealing the Hecht-Calandra Act, the 1971 state law that keeps the SHSAT as the only way into Stuyvesant, Bronx Science and Brooklyn Tech. Only Albany can change it.
  • Volunteer at a middle school near you. Check whether it offers Algebra I in eighth grade. If it does not, offer to tutor seventh and eighth graders in math, and tell your Community Education Council and your Council member.
  • Endorse the Dear Mamdani agenda.
