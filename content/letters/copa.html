Dear Mayor Mamdani,

On November 10 you signed the Community Opportunity to Purchase Act. COPA gives nonprofit groups the first chance to buy about 3,400 distressed and regulated buildings when they go up for sale. It is sold as a way to protect tenants. In practice it protects no one, and it will make life worse for the tenants it claims to help.

1. COPA was built for a market we don't have

A right of first offer makes sense in a hot market, where bidding wars push prices up and nonprofit buyers get shut out. That is not New York today. Regulation, capital and insurance costs have made owning these buildings unattractive. Rent-stabilized buildings now sell for 30% to 50% less than in 2019. Distressed buildings have traded at 70% to 90% below their earlier values. Since 2019, operating costs in stabilized buildings have risen 40% while rents rose 16%. Banks are selling off loans at a loss: Flagstar took an estimated $113 million write-down to sell the Pinnacle portfolio.

In a falling market, COPA doesn't protect nonprofits from being outbid. It lets them lock in a price before the market has time to correct. That is not protection. It is an open door to corruption.

2. Nothing stops nonprofits from buying today

Most owners of these buildings would love to sell. Almost nobody is buying. Sales of buildings that are more than 75% stabilized fell from $4.8 billion in 2015 to $1.1 billion in 2023. Lenders have pulled back too: banks surveyed by the Rent Guidelines Board made a third fewer new loans on these buildings last year, and many buyers now have to pay cash. So why aren't community organizations buying them now? They have no money. COPA gives them none. It doesn't solve that problem, or any other.

3. Who pays for the boiler?

Much is being said about tenants taking over failing buildings. Please explain how that would work. Where would tenants, who can't afford to move to a better building, find the money for new boilers, roofs, structural repairs, or to bring a building into compliance? Who will pay for it? There is no public money for this. COPA doesn't provide any, and the city hasn't committed a dollar of its preservation budget to COPA buildings. The buildings will simply go on suffering from mismanagement and disrepair, with the tenants still living in them. This proposition is absurd, and it will never work in practice.

My take

The problem in these buildings isn't who owns them. It's that the numbers don't work for anyone, and they stopped working in 2019.

The Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act of 2019 was an extinction-level wealth destruction event for New York landlords and developers. It outlawed nearly all legal ways to deregulate a building and capped what owners can recover for major capital investments so tightly that the work no longer pays for itself. There is no source of funding for necessary improvements. Owners' investments were wiped out, and now nobody wants to own or build here. Maverick Real Estate Partners estimated that rent-stabilized properties lost as much as $75 billion in value after 2019, according to Bloomberg's reporting in February 2024. By 2023, buildings with stabilized apartments were selling for 34% less per unit than in 2019, while prices for unregulated apartments rose 23%. Losses like these erase owners' equity and leave buildings worth less than their mortgages, even when the owners keep paying every bill.

Falling values and tighter lending now make buying, refinancing and reinvesting harder. In older stabilized buildings, built before 1974, the Rent Guidelines Board’s own data shows net operating income of $512 per apartment per month in 2024, about $6,100 a year. That is less than 3% of what these buildings sell for today, and far less on what owners paid before 2019. And that is before setting aside a dollar for roofs, boilers or façades. New loans cost about 6%, more for buildings in trouble, and many of those can't borrow at all. You can't fix a roof with money that costs more than the building earns. Nearly one in ten buildings with stabilized apartments already loses money before paying any mortgage.

Owners and tenants need support. Instead, you are adding to the regulatory burden with your signature smile. It's not funny. It is very sad, and it will make life even worse for the tenants in failing buildings.

What I'm asking you to do

  1. Offer property tax relief to any owner, nonprofit or private, who commits to major repairs: boilers, roofs, façades, gas lines and clearing violations.
  2. Guarantee repair loans for those buildings, so banks will lend for the work tenants need.
  3. If COPA stays, require nonprofits to show proof of funding when they express interest. Publish every COPA purchase with its price, its appraisal and the public money behind it.

The tenants in these buildings need working boilers, not a new owner who can't afford to buy one.

Sincerely,
Anya Levitov
