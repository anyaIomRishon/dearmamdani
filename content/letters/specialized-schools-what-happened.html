Dear Mayor Mamdani,

Every July the specialized high school offers come out, and every July the same headline follows: almost no Black students. This year Stuyvesant offered seats to 3 Black students. In 1975, 12% of its students were Black. At Brooklyn Tech, more than half the students were Black in 1982. The test has been around since 1971, and for years it didn't stop Black and Latino students from going to the specialized schools. What happened?

The city changed around the test.

The city lost its Black and Latino families

Between 2000 and 2020, New York gained about 800,000 residents and lost about 200,000 children. The loss was not shared equally.

The number of Black children in New York fell by more than a third. Black families left for more affordable suburbs in the South and the Midwest, priced out by rents and a shrinking supply of housing they could afford. Latino families followed, more slowly. At the same time, the number of Asian children more than doubled after 1990. The pool of children who could take the test looks nothing like it did a generation ago.

The pandemic made it worse. From 2020 to 2024, about 794,000 more people moved from the city to the rest of the country than moved in. Only immigration kept the population from falling further.

The city closed better options for Black and Latino students

In the 1990s, district after district in Black and Latino neighborhoods closed its gifted and accelerated programs, the classes that taught advanced math early and prepared children for the test. In one Bronx district, admissions to the specialized schools fell 80% in about ten years, while the district's population grew 25%.

The specialized schools felt it at once, and the numbers never came back. At Bronx Science, the Black and Hispanic share fell from 20% to 11% in the late 1980s and early 1990s. At Brooklyn Tech, the Black share fell from 24% in 2000 to 10% in 2012, and to 6% by 2016. Stuyvesant had 147 Black students in 1990, 109 in 2000 and 66 in 2005.

Families built their own path: charter schools

Families who wanted more for their children did not wait for the city. When New York allowed charter schools, they enrolled in them by the tens of thousands.

Today about 90% of charter students are Black or Latino, about 66,000 Black and 66,000 Latino children. And charters deliver. On the 2024 state tests, 61% of Black charter students were proficient in math, against 34% of Black students in district schools.

Charters start in kindergarten. They are in the neighborhood. Many run their own high schools. For a family that already has a good school, the specialized schools look less attractive: a long commute, a reputation for cutthroat competition, and an unnecessary change.

Many still try. This year 41% of charter 8th graders took the SHSAT, about the same share as the rest of the city. But only about 5% of charter test-takers got an offer, against 15% citywide. Proficiency in grade-level material doesn't guarantee acceptance to an accelerated school, where students are expected to know more than an average student who is doing well.

The private option shrank

Catholic schools once educated tens of thousands of Black and Latino children. Their enrollment in the city fell from about 134,000 in 2002–03 to about 77,000 by 2018–19, close to half. The number of Black students in private schools fell from about 52,000 in 2000 to about 18,000 today. Elite independent schools recruit some strong Black and Latino students, but they enroll about 50,000 students across all grades. They are a small part of the story.

My take

The test did not push Black and Latino children out of the specialized schools. Their families were priced out of the city. The city closed the programs that prepared the children who stayed. The families who remained built a separate system that starts in kindergarten, works well, and rarely leads to Stuyvesant.

Scrapping the test will not bring any of these children back. It will only hide the failure that happens years before 8th grade.

What I'm asking you to do

  1. Restore gifted and accelerated programs in every district, starting in elementary school, beginning with the Black and Latino neighborhoods that lost them.
  2. Offer Algebra I in 8th grade in every middle school, and work with charter networks to give their students free SHSAT preparation in 7th grade.
  3. Make the city affordable for families again by removing housing regulations and making it easier to build, so the next generation of children can grow up here.
  4. Keep the SHSAT. It is the one measure honest enough to show us where we are failing.

Sincerely,
Anya Levitov
