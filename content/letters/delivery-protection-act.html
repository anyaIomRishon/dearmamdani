Dear Mayor Mamdani,

When Amazon warned its New York customers this week that the Delivery Protection Act could add up to $5 to every order, you had a one-word answer: "greed." It's a good line. It is also the wrong diagnosis. The bill, Intro 518, sponsored by Council Member Tiffany Cabán, is sold as a fight against a trillion-dollar corporation. Amazon will be fine. The people who will lose are the small business owners who deliver its packages, the workers who will no longer have jobs, and the New Yorkers who will pay more for every order.

We don't have to guess. The city has already run this experiment, and the city's own data tells us how it ends.

1. The bill wipes out local businesses, not Amazon

Amazon does not deliver most of its packages in New York with its own employees. It contracts with local delivery companies, more than 40 of them, employing more than 5,000 people. Intro 518 would require operators of last-mile delivery stations to employ every driver directly. Those companies would lose their only customer.

These are not faceless corporations. By Amazon's count, a quarter of them are owned by Black or Hispanic New Yorkers and one in ten by veterans. Their owners have names: Jordan Rodriguez in the Bronx, Roman Dekhterman on Staten Island, Juan Martinez in Red Hook, Rudy Cazares, who said it better than anyone: "Amazon is going to be fine. People like us are the ones who are going to pay the price." One of them, DashWave, started in 2023 with 15 employees and now has more than 100. The bill doesn't regulate them. It erases them.

Supporters say the bill protects their drivers, and that Amazon controls the routes, the vans and the uniforms anyway. Then explain what changes on the street. The vans are already Amazon's. The packages are already Amazon's. The only thing that disappears is the small business in the middle, and the people who own it.

2. We already know what happens to the workers

In December 2023 the city set a minimum pay rate for restaurant delivery workers. It was sold the same way: protect the workers from greedy apps. Here is what the city's own October 2026 report shows, comparing mid-2023 with this spring:

  • The number of workers on the restaurant delivery apps fell from 106,931 to 69,806, a drop of 35%.
  • The number of workers who actually made deliveries fell from 75,865 to 67,500, a drop of 11%.
  • For grocery apps, covered since January, workers making deliveries fell 14% in a single year.

Those who kept their spots earn much more. Good for them. Everyone else was shut out. The apps now make workers book shifts in advance and let in only as many as they need. Your own Department of Consumer and Worker Protection admits that working across several apps, or logging in for a few deliveries when you need the money, has become "functionally impossible." The part-timer, the newcomer, the person who needed work this week: the door is closed. That is what protection looked like.

Now apply the same arithmetic to Amazon. If one in nine delivery jobs disappears, as it did for restaurant delivery, that is more than 550 people out of work, on top of every one of the 40-plus companies that employed them. Amazon will hire the drivers it needs. It will not hire the owners.

3. Deliveries won't drop. New Yorkers will pay.

Here is the part the bill's supporters never mention. After the pay rules, deliveries did not fall. Restaurant deliveries rose from 2.63 million a week to 3.39 million. Grocery deliveries stayed flat. New Yorkers kept ordering. They just paid more.

  • Instacart added a $5.99 "regulatory response fee" to every New York order days after the grocery rules took effect.
  • DoorDash charges its own "regulatory response fee" here, and raised its New York fees again this spring, citing the new minimum pay.
  • The average customer fee on a grocery delivery rose from $7.85 to $12.76 in a year, up 63%.
  • Tips fell. Total restaurant delivery tips dropped 24%. The average grocery tip fell from $6.86 to $4.22. Customers paying a fee to the app give less to the worker at the door.

The apps didn't eat the cost. They passed it on, kept their customers and kept their margins. Amazon will do exactly the same. Whether the number is $5 or $2, it will land on the people who order diapers, groceries and medicine to their door, including the elderly, the disabled and parents working two jobs. Not on Amazon.

My take

This is not an unintended consequence. It is a perfectly predictable one. The city already ran the experiment, published the results and called it a victory. More money for fewer workers, higher prices for every customer, and the companies the law was aimed at doing just fine. Now the Council wants to run it again, and this time the casualties are small businesses.

Calling it "greed" doesn't change the arithmetic. If you want to fight Amazon, fight Amazon. Don't do it by shutting down the Bronx and Red Hook businesses that work for it, and sending the bill to every New Yorker who orders a package.

What I'm asking you to do

  1. Withdraw your support for Intro 518, and say you will not sign it in its current form.
  2. Before the Council passes any new delivery mandate, have the city publish what it will cost customers and how many people will lose the ability to work, not just how much the ones who remain will earn.
  3. If the concern is street safety, write safety rules for every delivery operator and enforce them. Who signs the paycheck doesn't make a van drive slower.

Protecting workers means letting more of them work, not fewer.

Sincerely,
Anya Levitov
